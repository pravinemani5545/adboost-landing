Most health brands don't have a measurement problem, they have a hierarchy problem. The dashboard has forty numbers on it, every platform reports its own version of success, and when the monthly review comes around nobody can say which three numbers actually decide whether marketing is working.

CAC usually gets promoted to the top, and it deserves a seat. But CAC alone tells you what a patient cost, not whether that patient was worth it, why the cost moved, or what happens next month. This post lays out the healthcare marketing KPIs worth paying attention to, in the tiers that make them useful, plus the metrics to stop reporting, how to measure without creating a privacy problem, and a cadence for using the scorecard.

## What are healthcare marketing KPIs?

Healthcare marketing KPIs are the small set of metrics that tell you whether marketing is producing patients profitably, and why. A metric is anything you can measure; a KPI is a metric someone is accountable for and that changes a decision when it moves.

Healthcare marketing analytics generates far more metrics than any team can act on. The fix is to arrange them in layers, so every number has a job:

- **Outcome KPIs** answer "is marketing working for the business?" Reviewed monthly by whoever owns the P&L.
- **Funnel KPIs** answer "where is the patient journey leaking?" Reviewed weekly by marketing and intake.
- **Retention KPIs** answer "what is a patient worth, and how much can we afford to pay for one?"
- **Leading indicators** answer "what is about to change?" Reviewed weekly or more often by whoever runs media and creative.

When an outcome KPI moves, you walk down the layers to find the cause.

## Why isn't CAC enough on its own?

CAC is a cost, and a cost is only meaningful next to a value and a time frame. A $400 patient can be a bargain or a disaster depending on what the patient is worth and how fast they pay it back, and CAC alone can't tell you which.

We covered how to calculate a fully loaded number in our [patient acquisition cost guide](/blog/patient-acquisition-cost/). Even done perfectly, CAC has three blind spots:

- **It ignores value.** Two programs with identical CAC can have completely different economics if one retains patients for a year and the other for a month.
- **It's backward-looking.** By the time CAC rises, the cause (creative fatigue, a broken intake step, a competitor entering the auction) has usually been running for weeks.
- **It hides where the cost comes from.** CAC can rise because media got expensive, because the funnel converts fewer visitors, or because fewer booked patients show up. Each needs a different fix, and CAC alone won't say which one you're dealing with.

## Which outcome KPIs should leadership track?

Five outcome KPIs cover almost every health business, whether that's a telehealth program, a multi-location clinic, or a supplement brand.

### Blended CAC

Total acquisition spend divided by total new patients, across every channel including organic and referral. This is the version of CAC that judges the business, as opposed to paid CAC, which judges the ad account. See the [blended CAC definition](/glossary/blended-cac/) for how the two differ.

### Patient LTV

Patient lifetime value is the contribution margin a patient generates over their relationship with you, not the revenue. Use margin, because a $200 monthly subscription with $140 of medication, clinician, and fulfillment cost is a $60 patient-month, not a $200 one. For new programs without much history, use a conservative 6- or 12-month LTV rather than an extrapolated lifetime figure.

### LTV:CAC ratio

The relationship between the two numbers above. It's the cleanest single answer to "can we afford to grow?" Run your own in the [LTV:CAC calculator](/tools/ltv-cac-calculator/), and recompute LTV from actual cohorts every quarter: a strong ratio built on optimistic LTV is how health brands talk themselves into overspending.

### CAC payback period

How many months of contribution margin it takes to recover the cost of acquiring a patient. Payback is the cash-flow version of LTV:CAC: two brands with the same ratio can have very different growth capacity if one recovers CAC in six weeks and the other in eight months.

### Marketing efficiency (MER) and healthcare marketing ROI

Marketing efficiency ratio is total revenue divided by total marketing spend. It's crude, which is the point: attribution settings can't game it, and it catches every platform reporting better ROAS while the business is flat. Our [MER calculator](/tools/mer-calculator/) runs it from two numbers.

Healthcare marketing ROI goes one step further: contribution margin from new patients acquired in a period, minus the marketing cost of acquiring them, divided by that cost. Use margin rather than revenue here too, or ROI will look healthiest in exactly the programs with the thinnest margins.

## Which funnel KPIs explain why CAC moves?

Funnel KPIs are the diagnostic layer. When blended CAC rises, these tell you whether the problem is traffic cost, conversion, or follow-through. The patient acquisition metrics worth tracking, in journey order:

- **Landing page conversion rate.** Visitors who start the primary action (book, begin intake, call). Track it per landing page and per traffic source, because a blended site-wide rate hides the page that's leaking. For how to improve it, see our [healthcare CRO guide](/blog/healthcare-cro/).
- **Cost per qualified lead.** Not cost per lead. A lead who fails eligibility, is outside your service area, or wants a service you don't offer is not a lead you should pay to optimize toward. Define "qualified" once, in writing, and hold every channel to it.
- **Speed to lead.** Median time from inquiry to first human or automated response. For clinics and consult-led programs, this is often the single most controllable lever on lead-to-booking rate, and it's measured by your intake team, not your ad platform.
- **Lead-to-booked rate.** Qualified inquiries that become a scheduled consult or completed intake. A drop here with stable lead volume points at intake staffing, scheduling availability, or follow-up cadence, not at marketing.
- **Intake completion rate.** For telehealth: the share of patients who start an intake and finish it, through eligibility, history, ID verification, and payment. Measure it step by step so you can see which screen sheds patients.
- **Show rate and start rate.** Booked consults that actually happen, and consults that become a started treatment plan or first order. These are the last two steps between a "conversion" in your ad account and an acquired patient, and both are routinely missing from marketing reports.

This layer splits responsibility honestly: if click-through is flat but show rate fell, media isn't the problem.

## Which retention KPIs set your allowable CAC?

Retention KPIs don't change what a patient costs, they change what a patient is worth, which sets the maximum CAC you can pay and still grow profitably. For subscription and recurring-care models, they matter as much as anything on the acquisition side.

- **Cohort retention.** Of the patients acquired in a given month, the share still active at 30, 60, 90, and 180 days. Read it by cohort, never as one blended number, because a growing base of new patients makes blended retention look worse than it is (and a shrinking one makes it look better).
- **Rebill or refill rate.** For subscriptions and medication programs, the share of patients who successfully renew at each billing cycle. A rebill rate drop is often an operational issue (payment failures, pharmacy delays, a clinician follow-up that didn't happen) rather than a marketing one.
- **Net Promoter Score or a referral-rate proxy.** [NPS](https://www.netpromotersystem.com/about/measuring-your-net-promoter-score/) is the share of promoters (9-10 on a 0-10 "how likely are you to recommend us" scale) minus the share of detractors (0-6). Treat it as a trend that predicts referral volume, which lowers blended CAC.

The lifecycle side of this, including the email and SMS flows that move rebill and retention, is in our [retention and LTV playbook](/blog/health-brand-retention-ltv-email-sms/).

## Which leading indicators warn you before CAC rises?

Leading indicators move days or weeks before outcome KPIs do. Media and creative teams should watch them closely; leadership can mostly ignore them unless they persist.

- **CPM and frequency trends.** Rising CPMs at flat frequency usually means auction pressure; rising frequency with falling CTR usually means creative fatigue.
- **Hook rate and thumbstop on video.** The share of impressions that watch past the first few seconds. A falling hook rate on your top ads is the earliest signal that a creative refresh is due.
- **Creative win rate.** The share of new concepts that beat the current control. Near zero means your testing has stopped finding new angles.
- **Branded search volume.** Searches for your brand name are a rough proxy for demand your other channels are creating. When upper-funnel spend is cut and branded search falls a few weeks later, that's the connection last-click reporting hides.
- **New vs. returning patient mix.** Watch the share of "conversions" that are actually existing patients coming back. If it creeps up, your acquisition numbers are flattering you.

## Which healthcare marketing metrics are misleading?

Several popular metrics are fine as diagnostics and actively harmful as KPIs, because optimizing toward them produces activity instead of patients.

- **Cost per lead without a quality definition.** The cheapest leads are often the ones that never book.
- **Platform-reported ROAS or CPA, summed across platforms.** Each platform claims credit for the same patients, so the total exceeds reality. In-platform numbers are useful for comparing ads within one channel; cross-channel decisions belong to blended numbers. Our [healthcare marketing attribution guide](/blog/healthcare-marketing-attribution/) covers why platforms disagree and how to referee them.
- **Traffic and engagement.** Sessions, followers, and likes measure attention, not patients.
- **Average CAC as a scaling signal.** Average CAC across all spend can look healthy while the last 20% of budget is acquiring patients at two or three times the average. When deciding whether to scale a channel, look at marginal CAC: what the most recent increment of spend actually produced.

## How do you measure healthcare marketing KPIs without a privacy problem?

Build your KPIs from your own systems (CRM, EHR or practice management, billing) and use ad platforms and analytics tools only for aggregate, non-health data. Nearly every outcome and funnel KPI above can be calculated without sending anything sensitive to a third party.

The regulatory picture is specific enough to be worth stating carefully. In June 2024 a federal court [vacated part of HHS's online tracking guidance](https://www.aha.org/news/news/2024-06-20-judge-rules-favor-aha-vacating-hhs-online-tracking-bulletin-unlawful-and-beyond-agency-authority), the portion treating an IP address plus a visit to an unauthenticated public page about a health condition as protected health information. The guidance still applies to authenticated areas like patient portals and intake flows, which is exactly where conversion events tend to live. And brands outside HIPAA, including many health apps and DTC wellness companies, can fall under the FTC's [Health Breach Notification Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0), which treats unauthorized sharing of covered health information with a third party as a breach. This is not legal advice; have counsel review your tracking setup.

In practice, a compliant measurement setup for KPIs looks like:

- **Analytics configured for non-sensitive events.** In GA4, the actions you report on are marked as [key events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9267568?hl=en). Name them neutrally ("booking_started," not condition-specific names) and keep health details out of URLs, page titles, and event parameters.
- **Outcome data from your own record.** Bookings, shows, starts, and rebills come from your CRM or practice system, joined to a marketing source captured at booking.
- **A "how did you hear about us?" field** at booking or checkout, which costs nothing and fills gaps no pixel can.
- **Vendors that will sign a BAA** for anything that might touch patient information, such as call tracking or CRM.

## How should you structure a healthcare marketing KPI scorecard?

Keep it to one page, organized by review cadence, with a named owner for each line:

| Cadence | KPIs | Owner | Decision it drives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly | CPM, frequency, hook rate, creative win rate, cost per qualified lead, speed to lead | Media and intake leads | Creative refresh, bid and budget changes within a channel, intake staffing |
| Monthly | Blended CAC, landing page conversion, lead-to-booked, intake completion, show and start rate, MER | Head of growth | Budget allocation across channels, funnel fixes |
| Quarterly | Patient LTV by cohort, LTV:CAC, CAC payback, cohort retention, healthcare marketing ROI | Founder or P&L owner | Growth targets, allowable CAC, pricing and offer decisions |

A purely illustrative example of how the layers work together, with round invented numbers: a telehealth program sees blended CAC rise from $250 to $310 in a month. The monthly review shows landing page conversion and lead-to-booked unchanged, but show rate down from 70% to 58%. The weekly numbers show speed to lead roughly doubled after an intake coordinator left. It's a staffing and reminder problem, not a media problem, and cutting ad spend would have made it worse.

To know whether your outcome numbers are reasonable for your category, compare them against vertical ranges, like the ones in our [telehealth CAC benchmarks](/blog/telehealth-cac-benchmarks-2026/), rather than general ecommerce figures.

## Fewer numbers, clearer decisions

The point of healthcare marketing KPIs isn't more reporting, it's fewer arguments: outcome numbers everyone agrees on, a funnel layer that explains them, retention metrics that set the ceiling, and leading indicators that buy you warning. CAC is in there, but it no longer carries the scorecard alone.
