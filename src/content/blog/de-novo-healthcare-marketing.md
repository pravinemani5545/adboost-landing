A new clinic starts paying for itself on the day the lease is signed, not the day the doors open. Rent, build-out, equipment, and a hired team all start costing money before the first patient walks in, and every week the schedule sits half empty after opening is a week those fixed costs run against no revenue.

That is why de novo healthcare marketing is a timing problem before it is a channel problem. Most new locations get a "now open" banner, a few weeks of ads, and hope. This post covers the alternative: when each piece needs to start, how to time the budget, how to fill the schedule before opening day, how to build local awareness from zero, and how to judge the launch.

## What is de novo healthcare marketing?

De novo healthcare marketing is the work of building patient demand for a brand-new location (one built from scratch rather than acquired), starting months before it opens. The goal is to open with a booked schedule instead of an empty one, and to shorten the ramp from opening day to a location that covers its own costs.

Dental groups, physical therapy chains, med spas, urgent care operators, and specialty practices grow either by acquiring a practice (which comes with patients, reviews, and search history) or by opening a de novo location (which comes with none of those). An independent physician opening a first practice faces the same cold start: no search history or reviews, no local conversion data for ad platforms to learn from, no patient list, and no awareness among the people driving past the construction site. Each of those takes time to build, which is the whole argument for starting early.

## Why should healthcare marketing start before opening day?

Because the slowest parts of local healthcare marketing can't be rushed, and the cost of an empty schedule is highest in the first weeks. If marketing starts at opening, the location spends its most expensive period building foundations that could have been ready on day one.

Several things simply take calendar time:

- **Local listings.** Google Business Profile lets you [set a future opening date](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9174409?hl=en) up to a year ahead. Once the date is set and the business is verified, the profile appears on Google 90 days before opening, and you can publish posts, photos, and a description during that window. After opening, the profile carries a "Recently opened" tag for 90 days. Miss that window and you lose three months of visibility you could have had for free.
- **Organic search.** A location page published the week of opening has no chance of ranking that week. Published months earlier, it has time to get crawled, indexed, and linked from your other pages.
- **Referral relationships.** Referring physicians, local employers, and community groups don't send patients to a location they've never heard of. Those relationships take months.

De novo healthcare marketing also has an operational reason to start early. A pre-opening waitlist forces intake, scheduling, and front desk workflows to be live early, which is far cheaper than discovering a broken intake flow with real patients on the phone.

## When should marketing for a new clinic start?

New clinic marketing should start as soon as the lease is signed and the opening date is reasonably firm, usually four to six months out. The work then moves through four phases, each with a different job.

| Phase | Timing | Job | Key actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation | 6 to 3 months before | Make the location findable and measurable | Location page, listings, tracking, booking and waitlist setup |
| Demand building | 90 to 30 days before | Create awareness and capture early intent | Opening-date profile, local search, awareness creative, referral outreach |
| Conversion | 30 days before to opening | Turn interest into booked appointments | Pre-booking pushes, waitlist conversion, reminders, opening event |
| Ramp | Opening to 90 days after | Fill the schedule and build proof | Reviews, retargeting of non-bookers, budget rebalancing |

The exact timing shifts with the specialty. A med spa can open bookings weeks ahead; a primary care practice may need payer credentialing confirmed before it can honestly tell insured patients they're covered. Plan the calendar around the slowest dependency, not the fastest.

### Phase 1: foundation (six to three months out)

- **A real location page.** Address, map, opening date, services, providers (as hired), insurance accepted (once confirmed), hours, parking, and one call to action: join the waitlist now, book once scheduling opens.
- **Listings and NAP consistency.** Name, address, and phone number identical everywhere: Google Business Profile with the opening date, Apple Maps and Bing listings, and the healthcare directories relevant to your specialty.
- **Tracking before traffic.** Location-level UTMs, call tracking numbers per location, and conversion events for waitlist signups and bookings. Keep it HIPAA-aware: tracking stays on public marketing pages, no health information flows to ad platforms, and any vendor that may touch patient information should sign a BAA. Our [guide to telehealth attribution and server-side tracking](/blog/telehealth-attribution-server-side-tracking/) covers what can and can't be sent.
- **A booking path that works before opening.** Live scheduling for the first weeks, or a waitlist form that collects only contact details and consent to be contacted.

### Phase 2: demand building (90 to 30 days out)

- **Local search capture.** People already searching for your service nearby are the cheapest demand you will get. Run tightly geo-targeted non-brand search campaigns into the location page and waitlist.
- **Awareness creative.** Short video and image ads showing the actual building, the providers, and the access details: where, when, what it treats, how to get on the list.
- **Referral and community outreach.** Introductions to referring practices and local partners, ideally with a provider in the room.
- **Owned channels.** If you run other locations, tell existing patients who live closer to the new one.

### Phase 3: conversion (30 days out to opening)

Shift the message from "coming soon" to "book now." Open scheduling, contact the waitlist in waves, push first-month availability, and run an opening event if your specialty suits one.

### Phase 4: ramp (the first 90 days)

Fill schedule gaps and build the proof a new location lacks: reviews and a local track record. Rebalance budget weekly against what is booking, and retarget waitlist members who never scheduled.

## How should you time and budget a new location launch?

Budget a healthcare location launch as a curve, not a flat monthly number. Spend should start before opening, peak around the opening window, and settle toward a steady-state level as reviews, organic visibility, and referrals start doing part of the work.

Expect launch-period acquisition cost to run higher than a mature location's: no reviews, no organic traffic to blend CAC down, and campaigns still finding their footing. Judging a launch against a mature location's [blended CAC](/glossary/blended-cac/) will make a healthy launch look like a failure.

Here is a purely illustrative example, with round numbers invented to show the mechanics. Say a mature location in your group acquires new patients at a blended $150 and you want the new site at 100 new patients a month by month four.

- **Pre-opening (three months):** modest spend on search, awareness, and the waitlist, say $4,000 a month. The goal is waitlist volume and awareness, not patients.
- **Opening month:** the peak. Plan for 60 new patients at a launch CAC of $250 (no reviews yet, cold campaigns), so roughly $15,000.
- **Months two and three:** taper toward the steady-state CAC as reviews and organic visibility build.
- **Month four onward:** 100 patients at something closer to $150, so roughly $15,000, with less of it doing the heavy lifting.

The useful part is the shape: a pre-opening investment that produces no patients on paper, an expensive-looking peak, and a CAC decline that only happens if the foundation work was done. To build the curve from your own inputs, our [healthcare marketing forecasting guide](/blog/healthcare-marketing-forecasting/) walks through spend tiers and lag, and the [ad budget calculator](/tools/ad-budget-calculator/) gives you a quick first pass.

One constraint overrides all of this: capacity. Size opening-month spend to the appointments your hired providers can actually offer. The [capacity-based marketing playbook](/blog/capacity-based-healthcare-marketing/) covers how to match media to available appointments.

## How do you get bookings before a new clinic opens?

Give people a reason to commit early and a simple way to do it. The two workable mechanisms are a waitlist and advance scheduling, and most launches use both in sequence.

- **Waitlist first.** While the opening date is still moving, collect interest rather than appointments, then follow up with progress updates, provider introductions, and first access to scheduling.
- **Advance scheduling second.** Once the date and staffing are firm, open bookings, waitlist first.
- **Protect the booked schedule.** Appointments booked weeks ahead are exposed to more of life getting in the way. Build confirmations and reminders into the gap, and make rescheduling easy. Our post on [what patient no-shows do to CAC](/blog/patient-no-shows-cac/) covers why this matters for the numbers.
- **Don't promise what you can't confirm.** If a payer contract, provider start date, or service line isn't finalized, don't advertise it.

## How do you build local awareness for a new medical office?

Start with demand that already exists, then create more within a tight radius. For a physical location, targeting precision matters more than reach.

- **Get the geography right.** Google Ads location settings default to reaching people who are in, regularly in, or have [shown interest in your targeted locations](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722038). For a clinic that only serves people who can physically show up, the "Presence" option (people in or regularly in the area) usually fits better. Size the radius to real drive times, not round numbers.
- **Separate new-location campaigns.** Give the de novo site its own campaigns and budget. Folded into existing campaigns, the algorithm keeps spending where conversions already happen: your mature locations.
- **Lead with access, not outcomes.** Answer where, when, who, what it treats, and how to book, in the locals' language (neighborhoods, landmarks). It also keeps you clear of outcome claims.
- **Earn local press and partnerships.** A new clinic is often genuine local news, and that off-platform awareness makes paid ads land.

### Reviews: the proof a new location doesn't have

Google doesn't let users post reviews until the business is officially open, so every location starts at zero on opening day. Build a review request into the patient journey from week one: ask every patient, the same way, after the visit.

Two rules apply. Google's [Maps contribution policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) prohibits offering incentives such as discounts or free services in exchange for reviews, and the FTC's [rule on fake reviews and testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) bans fake reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, and incentives conditioned on positive sentiment. And when you reply to reviews, never confirm that the reviewer is a patient or discuss their care. Thank them generically and move any specifics offline.

## How should multi-location groups run de novo launches?

When de novo sites drive multi-location healthcare growth, treat each launch as a repeatable playbook: standardize the timeline, location page template, listings, campaign structure, and launch reporting, then adjust per market. Existing locations shorten the cold start with brand recognition, a patient list, and mature ad accounts. They're also a risk: a new site near an existing one can pull patients from it instead of creating new ones, so check radius overlap and watch the older location's bookings during the launch. ## How do you measure a new location launch?

Measure leading indicators before opening and booked, attended patients after, and compare the new location to its own plan rather than to mature locations. A launch is a ramp, so the question is whether the curve is bending the right way.

**Before opening:**

- Waitlist signups and cost per signup
- Location page conversion rate
- Google Business Profile views, calls, and direction requests
- Search impression share on local service terms

**After opening:**

- Booked appointments, and the share from the waitlist
- Show rate
- New patients per week against the ramp plan
- Location-level CAC, separate from the group
- Review count and slot utilization

Add a "how did you hear about us?" question at booking to catch what pixels miss: the drive-by sign, the physician referral, the community event. Our [healthcare marketing attribution guide](/blog/healthcare-marketing-attribution/) covers how to combine those answers with platform and CRM data.

## What are the most common de novo marketing mistakes?

- **Starting at opening.** The foundation work then happens when an empty schedule costs the most.
- **Advertising before operations are ready.** Calls to a phone nobody answers burn money and first impressions.
- **Judging the launch against mature locations.** Cutting spend in month two because it doesn't match a five-year-old clinic stalls the ramp.
- **Flat budgets.** They underfund the opening window and overfund the quiet months before it.
- **Over-promising.** Unconfirmed providers, services, or insurance create bad first visits and early negative reviews.
- **Ignoring cannibalization.** Counting every new-location patient as growth without checking where they used to go.
