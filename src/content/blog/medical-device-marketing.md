A cleared device, a strong clinical story, and a product that genuinely works are the entry ticket, not the growth plan. Plenty of medical device companies reach launch with all three and then stall, because the marketing was built for a generic consumer product or a generic B2B SaaS tool, and a medical device is neither.

Medical device marketing sits in an awkward middle. Your claims are bounded by what the device is cleared or approved to do. Your targeting is restricted the moment the ad platforms classify you as health. Your buyer might be a patient, a clinician, a purchasing committee, or all three in sequence. And your measurement has to work without sending health data to the platforms that are supposed to optimize your spend.

This guide covers what marketing needs to do for a device brand: buyers, claims, paid media, creative, conversion, and measurement.

## What is medical device marketing?

Medical device marketing is the work of creating and capturing demand for a regulated medical product, within the limits of its cleared or approved indications, across whichever buyers sit between the device and the patient. That last part is what separates it from most health marketing.

A telehealth brand sells a service to one person who both decides and pays. A device brand often has to persuade a patient who wants relief, a clinician who recommends or prescribes, and sometimes a facility or payer that writes the check. Each needs different proof, and a strategy that speaks to only one leaves the others to block the sale.

## Who are you actually selling to?

Before choosing a single channel, decide which buying model your device lives in. Most device brands fit one of three, and the marketing job changes completely between them.

### Consumer devices sold direct

Think wearables, monitors, pain relief and recovery devices, sleep products, and categories like hearing aids, where the FDA created an [over-the-counter category](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/hearing-aids/otc-hearing-aids-what-you-should-know) in October 2022 that lets adults with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss buy without a prescription or a fitting. These brands run closest to DTC ecommerce: the patient decides and pays, and the funnel ends in a checkout. The difference from ecommerce is that claims, targeting, and tracking all operate under health rules.

### Devices that need a clinician in the loop

Prescription devices, and devices that patients typically buy after a clinician recommends them, have a two-sided problem. Patient-facing marketing creates demand, but the sale closes only if a clinician agrees. Marketing has to arm the patient for that conversation and make sure the clinician has heard of you before the patient asks. Running only patient ads here produces a pile of interested people who hit a wall at the doctor's office.

### Capital and procedural devices sold to facilities

Imaging systems, surgical tools, and other devices bought by hospitals, surgery centers, and practices are a long-cycle B2B sale with clinical, financial, and operational stakeholders. Paid media here is not trying to produce a purchase. It is trying to produce qualified conversations for a sales team and keep the device visible to the committee across a buying cycle measured in months.

Brands that straddle two models need a separate funnel, budget line, and success metric for each, not one blended campaign talking to everyone.

## What rules shape medical device advertising?

In the US, two federal agencies split the job. According to a [GAO review of federal oversight of device advertising](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106197), the FDA oversees advertising for restricted devices (those that can only be sold on a licensed practitioner's authorization or under conditions set by regulation), while the FTC oversees advertising for devices that are not restricted. The same review counted 322 enforcement actions related to device advertising between 2018 and 2022, 255 from FDA and 67 from FTC. Enforcement in this category is not hypothetical.

Three practical consequences for marketing:

- **Claims stay inside the cleared indication.** What a device is "intended" to do is judged partly by how it is marketed, under FDA's [intended use regulation](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-801/subpart-A/section-801.4). Ad copy that drifts past the cleared indication, even in a UGC script or a comment reply, can create a regulatory problem, not just an ad rejection.
- **Substantiation comes before the ad, not after.** The FTC's [Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) explicitly covers health equipment and diagnostic tests, and requires competent and reliable scientific evidence for health claims, with randomized controlled trials described as generally the type of substantiation experts expect for health benefits. If you cannot point to the evidence for a claim, it does not go in the ad.
- **Risk information has to travel with benefits.** Copy that sells the upside without the material limitations is the kind of messaging regulators flag.

This is not legal advice, and every device brand should run ads through regulatory review. The marketing point: build a claims library first, each approved claim mapped to its evidence, and let creative work from it.

## How should paid media work for a medical device?

Paid media for medical devices works best when each channel has one job: search captures people already looking for a solution, social and video create demand among people who have the problem but not the vocabulary, and B2B channels keep clinicians and buyers warm. The mistake is expecting one channel to do all three.

### Search: capture problem-aware demand

Most patients do not search for your device by name. They search for the problem, the symptom, the activity they can no longer do, or the category ("over the counter hearing aids", "home TENS unit"). Search campaigns should be built around those problem and category queries, with landing pages that answer them directly, plus a branded campaign to protect the people your other channels already persuaded.

Expect constraints. Google lists health as a sensitive interest category under its [personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465), and advertisers promoting products in those categories cannot use advertiser-curated audiences. In practice, keyword intent and creative relevance do far more of the targeting work for a device brand than audience lists do.

### Social and video: create demand with the product itself

Devices have an advantage most health services do not: the product is visual. You can show it in use and show how it works without any claim beyond the cleared indication. Meta, TikTok, and YouTube put that demonstration in front of people who have not started searching. Targeting is restricted here too, so broad audiences plus strong creative is the usual model: a hook built around a specific daily frustration finds the people who have it.

### B2B channels: reach the clinician and the committee

For clinician-in-the-loop and capital devices, budget belongs on channels where clinicians and administrators spend professional attention: professional networks, specialty publications, conference presence, and retargeting of people who engaged with clinical content. The goal is recognition and qualified conversations, measured in pipeline rather than clicks. ## What creative works for medical devices?

The creative that works for medical devices shows the device solving a specific, recognizable problem inside its cleared indication, with enough proof that a skeptical buyer believes it. Polished brand films rarely do that on their own.

Angles worth building a testing system around:

- **Demonstration.** The device in real use, in real settings, in the first three seconds. Show setup, show the experience, remove the "will I be able to use this" doubt.
- **Mechanism.** A plain-language explanation of how it works. For devices, mechanism education often outperforms benefit claims because it is both more believable and easier to keep compliant.
- **Clinician authority.** A credentialed clinician explaining who the device is and is not for. This also does the work of arming patients for the clinician conversation.
- **User experience.** Real users describing what using it is like, with scripts reviewed against your claims library. Experience is safer ground than outcomes.
- **Objection handling.** Price, comfort, ease of use, insurance or HSA/FSA questions, return policy. Answering the objection in the ad filters out people who would never buy.

Volume matters because restricted targeting burns through winning ads faster. A structured approach to testing angles, hooks, and formats is covered in our [creative testing framework for health ads](/blog/creative-testing-framework-health-ads/). A pre-approved claims library is what lets a team ship compliant variants weekly instead of monthly.

## How do you turn clicks into device sales or qualified leads?

You turn clicks into sales by building a different conversion path for each buying model, then removing every step that does not help the buyer decide. Sending all traffic to a homepage built for investors and distributors is the most common leak we see.

- **Direct consumer devices** need a product page that does the selling the ad started: what it is, who it is for and not for, how it works, what it costs, what is in the box, return terms, and answers to the top objections. Then a short checkout.
- **Clinician-in-the-loop devices** need a path that prepares the patient for the clinician: a "talk to your doctor" kit, a provider locator if you have one, and a way to capture the lead so you can follow up when the appointment happens.
- **Capital and facility devices** need a demo or evaluation request flow with just enough qualification fields to route the lead correctly, plus gated clinical and economic content that sales can use.

In the clinician and B2B models, medical device lead generation depends on speed: slow follow-up is where inquiries go cold.

## How should medical device brands measure marketing?

Measure device marketing on business outcomes (units sold, qualified pipeline, cost per acquired customer and payback) rather than platform-reported conversions, because platform data is both incomplete and restricted for health advertisers.

The restriction is real. Meta's business help documentation on [prohibited information](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/361948878201809) states that the criteria advertisers set for events, conversions, and custom audiences must not reflect or be based on prohibited information, which includes health information, and that sharing it violates Meta's Business Tools terms. The same page notes Meta can place tools like the Meta Pixel into a restricted "core setup". So a device brand whose conversion events say "knee pain kit purchased" or whose URLs carry condition names is both breaking the rules and degrading its own optimization.

What a workable setup looks like:

1. **Neutral event names and clean URLs.** Generic events (purchase, lead, demo request) with no condition, symptom, or product-for-condition labels in event names, parameters, or URLs.
2. **Server-side tracking with data scrubbing.** Send conversions from your server so you control exactly what leaves, and strip anything health-related before it goes. Whether HIPAA applies depends on your setup and your relationships with providers, so involve counsel, but platform terms apply either way. Our glossary entry on [server-side tracking](/glossary/server-side-tracking/) explains the mechanics.
3. **Your own source of truth.** For DTC, your order system. For clinician and B2B models, your CRM, with closed revenue tied back to the campaigns that sourced or influenced it.
4. **A post-purchase or intake survey.** "How did you first hear about us?" catches the channels platform attribution misses, especially video and clinician word of mouth.

## What can a device brand afford to pay per customer?

A device brand can afford to pay up to the point where contribution margin from the customer pays back acquisition cost within a window its cash can carry. That answer depends heavily on whether revenue ends at the device sale or continues through consumables, subscriptions, or replacements.

Here is a deliberately round, illustrative example, not a benchmark. Say a consumer device sells for $300 with $150 of contribution margin after product cost, shipping, and payment fees. Half of buyers also subscribe to a consumable refill that contributes $20 a month and is kept for an average of 10 months.

- Device margin per customer: **$150**
- Consumable margin per customer: 50% × $20 × 10 months = **$100**
- Total contribution per customer: **$250**

If the brand wants to recover acquisition cost from first-order margin alone, its ceiling is about $150 per customer. If it is confident in the refill behavior and has the cash to wait, it can stretch toward $250, but every dollar above $150 is a bet on retention that must be measured, not assumed. Run your own version with the [LTV:CAC calculator](/tools/ltv-cac-calculator/).

For clinician-in-the-loop and capital devices, the same logic applies at the pipeline level: what you can spend per qualified opportunity is set by close rate, deal value, and margin, which is why tracking leads to closed revenue matters more than cost per lead.

## Where does medical device marketing usually go wrong?

Most medical device digital marketing failures happen before a single ad runs: one funnel for every buyer, claims reviewed after the creative is made instead of before, too few ads for a restricted-targeting category, budget chasing platform-reported conversions, condition-labelled tracking that breaks platform terms, and patient demand lost at the clinician. Fix them in order: buyer model, claims library, conversion paths, measurement, then creative volume and spend.
