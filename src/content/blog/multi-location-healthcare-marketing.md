A practice with one location has one marketing problem: get enough new patients through the door at a cost that works. A practice with six locations has six of those problems, plus a seventh: the locations start interfering with each other. Budgets get split evenly because that feels fair, ads for one clinic reach patients who live closer to another, and the monthly report shows one blended cost per patient that hides a clinic with a three-week waitlist next to one with half its schedule empty.

This post covers multi-location healthcare marketing from the paid media side: how to structure campaigns across locations, how to set budgets by location instead of by habit, how to stop clinics from competing with each other in the auction, what local presence each location needs, and how to measure patient acquisition per location without breaking HIPAA-aware tracking.

## What is multi-location healthcare marketing?

Multi-location healthcare marketing is running patient acquisition for a practice, group, or network with more than one physical site, so that each location gets the right volume of new patients at an acceptable cost while the brand stays consistent. The unit of planning shifts from "the practice" to "each location," while the brand, the tracking, and the account structure stay centralized.

Dental groups, physical therapy chains, med spa networks, and behavioral health groups all hit the same tension: patients choose a location, but marketing is usually planned, bought, and reported as one brand.

## Why does marketing break when a practice adds locations?

Because the averages stop meaning anything. A single blended cost per patient across five clinics can look healthy while two clinics are overspent, one is underfunded, and one is quietly absorbing patients that another location's ads paid for.

The common failure points:

- **Even budget splits.** Dividing spend equally ignores that locations differ in market size, competition, capacity, and maturity. A clinic in a dense metro and a clinic in a small town have completely different search volume to buy.
- **Overlapping geographies.** Two locations ten miles apart with 15-mile radius targeting are bidding on the same patients. You pay twice for one audience, and each campaign's reporting claims credit.
- **Brand search with no owner.** When every location campaign includes the brand name, they compete in the same auctions and the account bids against itself.
- **Capacity mismatch.** Marketing fills the schedule at the location with the best ads, not the location with open chairs. The busy clinic builds a waitlist while the quiet one stays quiet.
- **Reporting by platform, not by location.** Google and Meta report campaigns. The business needs new patients by clinic, and most accounts can't produce that number without a manual export.

None of these show up on day one. They compound with every location you add.

## How should you structure paid media across locations?

Structure so that every location's spend and results can be read on their own, while shared elements (brand, creative, tracking) are managed once. In practice that means each location owns its geography, and the account is organized so you can see cost per patient by location without stitching reports together.

There are two common structures, and the right one depends on how many locations you have and how different their service mix is.

| Structure | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Campaign per location | Each clinic gets its own non-brand campaigns, geo-targeted to its catchment area, with its own budget | Fewer locations, different service mixes, uneven capacity |
| Campaign per service line, segmented by location | Campaigns organized by service, with location targeting and location assets doing the local work | Many locations offering the same services, where budget control by clinic matters less |

Campaign per location gives the most budget control, at the cost of thinner conversion data per campaign, which matters for automated bidding. Past a handful of locations, grouping by service line or region usually becomes more practical.

### Google Ads settings that matter for multi-location practices

- **Location targeting option.** Google's default is "Presence or interest," which can show your ads to people who have only shown interest in a location. Google's [advanced location options](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722038?hl=en) also offer "Presence," which limits ads to people who are, or regularly are, in the area. For clinics that need patients to physically show up, Presence is usually the safer starting point.
- **Location assets.** Linking your Google Business Profile lets ads show the address, distance, a call button, and map details. Google notes that [location assets](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404182?hl=en) can appear on Search and alongside results on Google Maps, which is where a lot of "near me" healthcare decisions happen.
- **Location groups and label filters.** When all your profiles sync into one account, each campaign can show every clinic's address. Google's [location groups and Business Profile filters](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/9288588?hl=en) let you restrict which locations sync, including by labels you assign in Business Profile, so a campaign for one clinic shows that clinic's address.

Once each location's search account is built, it will eventually hit its own volume limit. A small-town clinic hits it far sooner than a metro one. We covered how to recognize that point in [what happens when healthcare paid search stops scaling](/blog/healthcare-paid-search-scaling/).

### Paid social across locations

On Meta and TikTok, use radius or city targeting around each clinic, split by location when budgets need separate control. The harder part is creative: ads that name the neighborhood or show the actual clinic and clinicians feel more relevant than generic brand ads, and they filter out people who live too far away to book.

Health advertisers also have less targeting to work with than most categories. Google's [health in personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en) bars customer match, your data segments, lookalikes, and audience expansion for ads promoting personal health content, while location targeting and predefined Google audiences remain available. When geography is one of the few reliable levers you have, getting location targeting right matters more, not less.

## How do you set marketing budgets by location?

Set them by each location's capacity and marginal cost per patient, not by an even split or by last year's numbers. A location should get budget up to the point where it either runs out of open appointments or the next patient costs more than you can afford.

A simple way to do this is to tier locations by maturity and constraint:

- **Capacity-constrained locations.** The schedule is full or close to it. Spend here should hold steady or come down, because more patients become waitlists, longer lead times, and more no-shows. (Long waits are one of the drivers we covered in [how patient no-shows inflate CAC](/blog/patient-no-shows-cac/).)
- **Growth locations.** Open capacity, proven demand, acceptable cost per patient. These are where incremental budget earns the most.
- **New locations.** No track record, no reviews, and often low brand awareness in the area. These need a launch budget with its own expectations: higher early cost per patient, a longer ramp, and a review date rather than an immediate verdict.

### A worked example

Here's an illustration with deliberately round numbers, not benchmarks. A group runs four clinics on a $40,000 monthly paid media budget, split evenly at $10,000 each.

| Location | Spend | New patients | Cost per patient | Open capacity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clinic A | $10,000 | 50 | $200 | Almost none |
| Clinic B | $10,000 | 40 | $250 | Moderate |
| Clinic C | $10,000 | 25 | $400 | High |
| Clinic D | $10,000 | 45 | $222 | High |

The blended number is $40,000 for 160 patients, or $250 per patient. It looks fine, and it hides almost everything useful.

Clinic A is full, so its last few thousand dollars are buying patients it can't see promptly. Clinic D has room and converts well, so it's underfunded. Clinic C has the worst cost per patient and the most open capacity, which calls for a diagnosis before more budget: weak search demand in that market, an overlapping radius with another clinic, a thin review profile, or a front desk that doesn't answer new-patient calls quickly. Moving part of A's budget to D, and holding C flat while the cause is fixed, is a better plan than another even split, and the blended average would never have shown it.

## How do you stop locations from competing with each other?

Give every location an exclusive geography and give brand search a single owner. Most internal competition comes from overlapping radii and brand terms that every campaign bids on at once.

- **Draw catchment areas, not circles.** Where locations are close together, replace overlapping radii with ZIP codes, cities, or neighborhoods assigned to one clinic each. Where two locations share a natural market, accept that and run them as one regional campaign with both addresses.
- **Centralize brand search.** Run one brand campaign for the practice name, and let location assets and the landing page route patients to the nearest clinic. Exclude brand terms from location-level non-brand campaigns so they don't bid on the same queries.
- **Route on the landing page.** A patient searching your brand name isn't choosing a location yet. A location finder, or a booking flow that asks for ZIP code first, does the routing better than a dozen campaigns fighting for the click.
- **Watch shared terms.** Generic searches like "physical therapy near me" will match several location campaigns if geographies overlap. Check the search terms report by campaign for duplicated queries after any targeting change.

## What local presence does each location need?

Each location needs its own verified Google Business Profile, its own landing page, and consistent name, address, and phone details everywhere it appears. Paid media sits on top of local presence; it doesn't replace it.

- **A verified Business Profile per location.** Google lets businesses with [10 or more locations](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3217744?hl=en) add, verify, and manage them in bulk through a business group and a spreadsheet upload. Smaller groups verify each location individually. Either way, location assets in Google Ads depend on these profiles being accurate.
- **A real location page.** Not a template with the city name swapped in. Address, hours, the services offered at that site, the clinicians who practice there, parking or transit details, insurance accepted, and a booking path that goes straight to that clinic's schedule.
- **Consistent details.** Hours and phone numbers that differ between the profile, the website, and the ad create calls to the wrong clinic and bookings that never happen.
- **Reviews collected honestly, location by location.** A new clinic with no reviews converts worse than an established one. Ask every patient and make it easy; never incentivize or filter.

Location pages also feed how AI assistants and search engines describe your practice. We cover that side in [AI search visibility for health brands](/blog/ai-search-visibility-health-brands/).

## How do you measure marketing performance by location?

Make location a field on every lead and patient record, then report new patients, cost per patient, and show rate by location from your CRM or practice management system, not from the ad platforms. Platform reporting shows campaigns; the business decisions are about clinics.

What to track per location:

- **New patients, not leads.** A form fill or a booked call isn't a patient. Report attended first visits by clinic.
- **Fully loaded cost per patient.** Media spend allocated to that location, plus a share of shared costs. Then roll up to [blended CAC](/glossary/blended-cac/) for the practice as a whole.
- **Speed to answer and booking rate.** One front desk converting inquiries far worse than the others looks like a marketing problem in the ad account and isn't one.
- **Open capacity.** Next-available appointment date per location is the simplest leading indicator for where budget should go next.

Multiple locations also make incrementality testing easier than it is for a single clinic. Pausing or increasing spend in some markets while holding others steady is a natural geo holdout, which answers whether ads are producing new patients or claiming ones who would have booked anyway. The [healthcare incrementality guide](/blog/healthcare-incrementality/) walks through how to design one.

### Keep location tracking HIPAA-aware

More locations means more pages, booking widgets, and tags. Conversion events should describe actions (booked, attended), not conditions, and nothing identifying a patient's health should reach ad platforms. A 2024 federal court decision vacated part of HHS's online tracking guidance, and HHS later withdrew its appeal, as [summarized by Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240930-a-mofo-privacy-minute-q-a-hhs-withdraws). That didn't make every setup safe, especially on authenticated pages and booking flows, and state privacy laws add their own rules. Have counsel review the tracking plan.

## What should be centralized and what should stay local?

Centralize anything that benefits from scale or consistency; keep local anything that depends on knowing the clinic. Splitting it the wrong way gives you either a generic brand with no local relevance or six mini-brands with no consistency.

- **Centralize:** account structure, bidding, and budget allocation; brand search; creative production and compliance review; tracking and reporting; landing page templates and the booking flow.
- **Keep local:** clinician bios and photos, clinic details, review requests and responses, referral relationships, and input on capacity, staffing changes, and service availability.

The capacity input is the one most practices skip. If marketing doesn't know a clinic just lost a provider, it keeps buying patients that clinic can't see.
