Most practices treat their patient no-show rate as an operations problem. The front desk owns it, the reminder software owns it, and marketing reports a "booked appointment" as a win the moment the calendar slot fills. Then the patient doesn't show, nobody tells the ad account, and the campaign that produced that booking keeps getting credit for a patient who never existed.

That gap is expensive. Every first-appointment no-show is acquisition spend that bought nothing, and if your reporting stops at "booked," your patient acquisition cost is wrong in the most flattering direction. Below: how to calculate the no-show rate, what the research says about typical rates, how no-shows inflate CAC (with a worked example), how they distort what your ad platforms optimize on, and which marketing decisions push them up or down.

This applies to anyone whose acquisition funnel ends in a scheduled visit: clinics, med spas, fertility practices, TRT and hormone programs, mental health providers, and telehealth brands running synchronous consults. If your patients convert through an asynchronous intake with no appointment, the same logic applies to abandoned intakes, which we cover in the [telehealth landing page conversion guide](/blog/telehealth-landing-page-conversion/).

## What is a patient no-show rate?

The patient no-show rate is the share of scheduled appointments where the patient neither attended nor cancelled in advance. The formula: **no-shows ÷ scheduled appointments in the period × 100**.

Two definitions decide whether the number is useful:

- **Separate no-shows from late cancellations.** A patient who cancels two hours before the visit leaves the same empty slot as one who never appears, but they behave differently and respond to different fixes. Track both, report them side by side, and decide in advance what counts as "late."
- **Separate first appointments from established-patient visits.** This is the distinction that matters for marketing. A missed follow-up is an operations and retention issue. A missed *first* appointment is an acquisition failure: you paid to generate that booking, and it never became a patient. Blending the two hides the acquisition problem inside a practice-wide average.

For the rest of this post, "no-show rate" means the new-patient, first-appointment no-show rate, because that's the one wired directly into CAC.

## What is the average patient no-show rate?

There is no single benchmark, because rates swing widely by specialty, population, and how a practice defines a no-show. The research gives useful reference points, not a target.

- A [systematic review of 105 studies in Health Policy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29482948/) found an average no-show rate on the order of 23%, ranging from 13.2% in Oceania to 43% in Africa. The same review found the most commonly reported determinants were long lead time (the gap between booking and the appointment) and a prior history of no-shows.
- A [study across Veterans Affairs clinics in BMC Health Services Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714455/) found a mean no-show rate of 18.8% across ten clinics, ranging from 12.6% in audiology to 25.7% in gastroenterology, and estimated an average cost of $196 per missed appointment (in 2008 dollars).
- The direction of travel isn't encouraging. In an [MGMA Stat poll from August 2026](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stat/about-1-in-3-medical-groups-see-higher-no-shows-in-2026-as-patients-face-higher-costs), 32% of medical group leaders said no-show rates were higher year to date than in 2025, 58% said about the same, and 10% said lower. Respondents pointed to patient cost pressure, transportation, and work conflicts.

Two caveats. First, most published rates are practice-wide, mixing new and established patients, and new-patient visits often behave differently because there's no existing relationship holding the slot in place. Second, the $196 figure is an operational cost of the empty slot. It does not include the acquisition cost of the patient who never showed, which is the cost this post is about.

The practical move is to benchmark against yourself: your new-patient no-show rate this quarter versus last, by channel and by campaign.

## How do patient no-shows affect CAC?

No-shows raise your real patient acquisition cost because they shrink the denominator. CAC is total acquisition spend divided by new patients acquired, and a booking that doesn't show is not an acquired patient. Same spend, fewer patients, higher CAC.

The trap is that many practices calculate CAC on bookings. That number looks great, and it's measuring the wrong thing. We made the same point in the [patient acquisition cost guide](/blog/patient-acquisition-cost/): divide by patients, not leads, and a booked consult that no-shows is still a lead.

### A worked example

Deliberately round, illustrative numbers, not benchmarks. A clinic spends $60,000 a month on fully loaded acquisition (ads, agency, tooling, and intake labor) and generates 400 booked new-patient consults.

| Scenario | First-visit no-show rate | Attended consults | Cost per booked consult | Cost per attended consult |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 20% | 320 | $150 | $188 |
| No-shows creep up | 30% | 280 | $150 | $214 |
| No-shows climb further | 40% | 240 | $150 | $250 |

Look at the "cost per booked consult" column. It doesn't move. A dashboard reporting cost per booking would show this clinic as perfectly stable while its real cost per attended consult rises by a third. Then apply the consult-to-patient close rate on top, and the gap compounds: if half of attended consults start treatment, true CAC goes from $375 to $500 between the first and last rows, on identical media performance.

### The costs that don't show up in the ad account

The CAC hit is only the visible part. A first-appointment no-show also burns **intake labor** (someone answered the inquiry, checked eligibility, booked the slot, and sent reminders, and that cost sits in your acquisition numerator either way), **clinical capacity** (the empty slot could have gone to another new patient you already paid to acquire), and **recovery effort** (rebooking takes outreach, and some of those patients never come back at all).

## How do no-shows distort your marketing data?

No-shows don't just raise CAC, they corrupt the signal your ad platforms learn from. If the conversion event you optimize toward is "appointment booked," the algorithm learns to find people who book, not people who attend. Those are not the same audience.

Three distortions follow.

- **Campaigns optimize toward bookers.** A campaign whose offer attracts casual, low-commitment bookings will look like your best performer on cost per booking while delivering your worst cost per attended patient. The platform will keep scaling it, because by the only measure you gave it, it's winning.
- **Channels get misranked.** One channel can book cheaply and show poorly; another can book expensively and show reliably. Rank them on cost per booking and you'll move budget in exactly the wrong direction. This is the same problem we describe in [healthcare marketing attribution](/blog/healthcare-marketing-attribution/): the measurement lens you choose decides which channel looks like the winner.
- **Lead-quality problems hide in operations.** When the no-show rate climbs, it reads as a front-desk issue. Often it's a targeting or offer issue upstream, and nobody connects the two because the data lives in different systems.

The fix is to measure show rate by source and, where you can, feed attended-visit outcomes back into your optimization. That second part needs care in healthcare. An attended appointment is health-adjacent data, and ad platforms won't sign a BAA, so nothing that identifies a patient or reveals a condition should flow to them. A HIPAA-aware setup sends only a minimal, non-identifying conversion signal from your own systems, and some practices decide the safer answer is to keep show-rate analysis entirely in their CRM. Our guide to [server-side tracking for telehealth attribution](/blog/telehealth-attribution-server-side-tracking/) covers what can and can't be sent.

## Which marketing decisions drive patient no-shows up?

Several of the strongest no-show drivers are set by marketing before the patient ever talks to the front desk. The research consistently points to lead time and prior no-show history as top predictors, and marketing influences both the lead time and the kind of patient who books.

- **Frictionless booking of low-intent clicks.** One-tap booking is good for conversion rate, and it also lets people book on impulse with little commitment. The fix isn't to add friction everywhere, it's to make sure the booking step follows enough qualification that the patient knows what they're booking.
- **Offers that attract bookers, not patients.** "Free consultation" and heavy discount hooks can fill a calendar with people shopping, not deciding. Free consults aren't wrong, but their show rate needs to be measured separately from paid or deposit-backed bookings.
- **A gap between the ad promise and the appointment.** If the ad implies a same-week prescription and the consult turns out to be an eligibility screening, patients who figure that out beforehand stop showing up. Ad, landing page, and appointment confirmation should describe the same thing.
- **Long lead times created by campaigns.** Scaling spend faster than clinical capacity pushes new bookings further out, and higher lead time is one of the most consistent no-show predictors in the literature. A campaign that fills next month's calendar can raise CAC even while its booking numbers look excellent.
- **Price surprises.** If cost, insurance coverage, or out-of-pocket expectations first appear after booking, some patients quietly opt out by not showing. Given the cost pressure MGMA respondents describe, transparency up front protects show rate.
- **Targeting too far from the clinic.** For in-person practices, distance from the clinic is associated with higher no-show rates in the Health Policy review. Broad geographic targeting can buy bookings that were never likely to make the drive.
- **Slow speed to lead.** When an inquiry waits hours for a callback, the patient's intent cools before the booking is even made, and a cooled booking is more likely to slip. What happens between the click and the booked visit is its own discipline, covered in our [healthcare lead generation guide](/blog/healthcare-lead-generation/).

## How do you reduce patient no-shows?

Combine the proven operational basics with marketing-side fixes. Reminders are the best-studied intervention, but they work on the bookings you already have. Marketing decides what kind of bookings arrive in the first place.

### The operational basics

- **Multiple reminders, not one.** A [meta-analysis in BMJ Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/) of 21 randomized studies found patients who received electronic text notifications were 25% less likely to no-show (15% versus 21%), and multiple notifications were significantly more effective than a single one. An earlier [meta-analysis in Health Services Research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22091980/) found SMS reminders substantially increased attendance across randomized trials.
- **Make cancelling and rescheduling easy.** A patient who can reschedule in one tap frees the slot for someone else. A patient who has to call during business hours often just doesn't show.
- **Shorten lead time for new patients.** Hold capacity for first visits so new bookings land within days, not weeks. Given how strongly lead time predicts no-shows, this can matter more than any reminder cadence.
- **Consider deposits or card on file, carefully.** MGMA reports that 42% of practices used a no-show fee in January 2025, and practices with a fee were more likely to report improving no-show rates. Fees and card-on-file policies can improve show rate, and they can also reduce booking volume and feel hostile. Test them, and measure cost per attended patient, not bookings, to judge the result.

### The marketing-side fixes

Each driver in the previous section has a mirror-image fix: qualify before the calendar with a short step that explains what the visit is and what it costs, keep ad, landing page, confirmation, and reminders describing the same appointment, match ad radius to the distance patients will actually travel, and pace spend to clinical capacity so lead times don't stretch. Add one more: a short pre-visit message explaining what happens in the first visit and who the clinician is, which builds commitment between booking and attending. Fewer bookings at a higher show rate often means lower CAC.

## How should you report CAC when no-shows are in play?

Report the full funnel, and make cost per attended patient the number that decides budget. A practical metric stack:

1. **Cost per lead**: spend divided by inquiries. Useful for diagnosing creative and targeting, not for judging the business.
2. **Cost per booked appointment**: the number most dashboards stop at.
3. **First-visit show rate**: attended first appointments divided by booked, reported by channel, campaign, and offer.
4. **Cost per attended consult**: spend divided by attended first visits.
5. **Patient acquisition cost**: spend divided by patients who actually start care. This is the number that should be compared against lifetime value, ideally as [blended CAC](/glossary/blended-cac/) across all channels.

Show rate is the bridge between marketing and operations metrics, which is why it belongs in the marketing report, not just the front-desk report. If you're building out the broader scorecard, our post on [healthcare marketing KPIs beyond CAC](/blog/healthcare-marketing-kpis/) covers what else belongs on it.

The diagnostic rule: if cost per booking is flat and cost per attended patient is rising, look at no-shows before you touch bids, budgets, or creative. The ad account may be doing exactly what you asked. You asked for the wrong thing.

## FAQ

### How do you calculate patient no-show rate?

Divide no-shows by scheduled appointments in the period and multiply by 100. For marketing, calculate it on new-patient first appointments separately from established-patient visits, and track late cancellations next to it, because a missed first appointment is the acquisition failure that feeds directly into CAC.

### What is the average patient no-show rate?

There is no single benchmark. A systematic review of 105 studies found an average on the order of 23%, and a study of Veterans Affairs clinics found a mean of 18.8%, with wide ranges by specialty and region. Most published rates mix new and established patients, so benchmark your own new-patient rate by channel.

### How do patient no-shows affect patient acquisition cost?

They raise it. Patient acquisition cost is spend divided by patients acquired, and a booking that never shows is not an acquired patient. Cost per booked consult stays flat while cost per attended consult climbs, which is why CAC calculated on bookings looks better than reality.

### Do late healthcare appointment cancellations count as no-shows?

Not in the formula, but they cost almost as much. A patient who cancels two hours before the visit leaves the same empty slot as one who never appears. Track late cancellations separately, report them side by side with no-shows, and decide in advance what counts as late.

### How can marketing help reduce patient no-shows?

Qualify before the calendar, keep the ad, landing page, confirmation, and reminders describing the same appointment, match ad radius to how far patients will actually travel, and pace spend to clinical capacity so lead times stay short. Then judge the results on cost per attended patient, not bookings.

## No-shows are acquisition spend that didn't land

A missed first appointment isn't a scheduling inconvenience that happens after marketing's job is done. It's the last step of the acquisition funnel failing, and it shows up in your CAC whether or not your dashboard admits it. Measure show rate by source, optimize toward attended patients instead of bookings, and treat lead time, offer design, and message match as marketing levers, because they are.
