For most of the last twenty years, finding a healthcare provider followed a predictable script. A patient typed "physical therapist near me" or "dermatologist that takes Aetna" into Google, scanned the map pack, opened three or four tabs, read some reviews, and called whoever looked credible and had an opening. Healthcare search marketing was built around that script: rank in the map pack, rank for the service page, make the phone number easy to tap.

That script is being rewritten. Patients now ask full questions instead of typing keywords, and they increasingly get a synthesized answer back instead of a list of links. "Who's a good pelvic floor therapist near downtown Toronto that does virtual follow-ups?" is a different kind of search than "pelvic floor therapy Toronto," and the answer it produces is a short list of names with reasons attached. If your practice isn't in that list, the patient may never see your website at all.

We've already covered how [health brands get cited and recommended by ChatGPT and Perplexity](/blog/ai-search-visibility-health-brands/), which is the brand-level side of this shift. This post is about the patient side: how AI search is changing the way people research and choose a specific provider, what the answers are built from, and what clinics, practices, and telehealth providers should actually change.

## How are patients using AI search to find healthcare providers?

Patients are using AI search to do the research step that used to take four browser tabs: describing their situation in plain language, asking for options, and asking follow-up questions about fit, insurance, and logistics before they ever click through to a provider's website.

The adoption numbers are real, if still early. A [KFF poll published in August 2024](https://www.kff.org/health-misinformation-and-trust/poll-finding/kff-health-misinformation-tracking-poll-artificial-intelligence-and-health-information/) found that about one in six U.S. adults (17%) used AI chatbots at least once a month for health information and advice, rising to 25% of adults under 30. That survey predates much of the AI search rollout inside Google itself, which is where most patients still start.

The more important change is on the search results page. [Pew Research Center analysed the browsing data of 900 U.S. adults in March 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) and found that 18% of their Google searches produced an AI summary. When a summary appeared, users clicked a traditional search result in 8% of visits, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. Clicks on links inside the summary itself happened in just 1% of visits.

For a provider, the implication is straightforward. A growing share of patient research now ends on the results page or inside a chat window. The patient still chooses a provider; they just do more of the comparing before they reach you.

## What has changed about patient search behavior?

Three things have changed, and each one affects how a practice gets found.

- **Queries got longer and more specific.** Patients now describe their whole situation: the condition, the constraint, the preference. "Sports medicine doctor for a recurring hamstring injury, weekend hours, near Midtown" bundles four filters into one question. The answer will favour providers whose public information clearly addresses those filters.
- **The comparison happens before the click.** In the old model, the patient compared providers by opening their websites. In the AI model, the comparison is done for them, using whatever the engine could find. Your website still matters, but as a source the engine reads, not only as a destination the patient visits.
- **Follow-up questions replace navigation.** Instead of clicking through to your insurance page, the patient asks "do any of these take Blue Cross?" If the answer isn't written down somewhere retrievable, the engine either guesses, hedges, or drops you from the shortlist.

None of this removes the classic trust check. What has moved is the point at which patients form a shortlist, and the inputs it is built from.

## What do AI answers draw on when recommending a provider?

AI answers about providers are assembled from the same public information a careful patient would check: your Google Business Profile and other listings, your reviews, your own site's service and clinician pages, and third-party directories and articles that mention you. There is no separate AI database you submit to.

Google is explicit about this for its own AI features. Its [guidance on AI Overviews and AI Mode](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says there are "no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary." A page has to be indexed and eligible to show in Search with a snippet. Google also describes a "query fan-out" technique, where the system runs multiple related searches across subtopics to build a response. In practice, one patient question about a provider can trigger searches about the service, the location, the insurance, and the reviews, and your practice needs to be legible in each of them.

For local discovery specifically, Google's own documentation on [how local results are ranked](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) names three factors: relevance, distance, and prominence. Prominence includes how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have, and Google states that more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking. Those are the same signals that tend to show up in the reasoning of an AI answer ("highly rated," "well reviewed for post-surgical rehab").

Outside Google, assistants like ChatGPT search the live web when answering. OpenAI's [crawler documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots) says OAI-SearchBot is used to surface websites in ChatGPT's search features, and sites that block it won't appear in ChatGPT search answers.

## Why does health content face a higher bar in AI search?

Health information falls into the category Google treats with the most caution, so the trust bar for being surfaced is higher than for most industries. Google's [guidance on helpful, reliable content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) says its systems "give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people," and that of experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness, "trust is most important."

For a provider, that translates into a few concrete properties:

- **Named, credentialed clinicians.** A service page that says "our experienced team" gives an engine nothing to cite. A page that names the clinician, their credentials, their training, and what they treat gives it something specific to attach to your practice.
- **Accurate, conservative claims.** AI answers can quote your sentences outside the context of your page. A line that overstates outcomes reads worse when it is lifted into an answer, and it can create compliance exposure. The same claim discipline you apply to ads applies here.
- **Consistency across sources.** If your website says you're open Saturdays, your Business Profile says you're not, and a directory lists an address you left two years ago, the engine has conflicting information about you. Conflicting information makes you a weaker candidate for a confident recommendation.

The same trust signals that decide whether an AI engine names you also decide whether a patient books once they find you. ## What should healthcare providers change for AI search?

Most of the work is unglamorous: make the facts patients ask about easy to find, consistent, and attributable to a real clinician. Here is the order we'd work in.

### 1. Fix your listings and profile data first

Your Google Business Profile is usually the first source read about a local practice. Check every field: categories, services, hours (including holiday hours), accessibility details, booking link, and the description. Then check the directories that matter for your specialty and make sure name, address, phone, and hours match exactly. This is the cheapest, highest-leverage step, and it is the one most practices have let drift.

### 2. Write the answers to the questions patients actually ask

Collect the questions your front desk answers ten times a day. Do you take my insurance? Do I need a referral? How long is the wait for a new patient? Do you offer virtual visits? What does the first appointment involve? Then make sure each one is answered in plain text on your site, ideally on the relevant service or location page, with a question-style heading and a direct answer underneath. These are exactly the follow-up questions patients now ask AI assistants, and an engine can only answer them for you if you've written the answer down.

### 3. Build one real page per service and per clinician

A single "Services" page with a bulleted list of twenty treatments is hard for any system to match against a specific question. A dedicated page for each core service (what it treats, who it's for, what to expect, who provides it, where) gives the engine a specific source for a specific query. Clinician pages should carry real credentials and real areas of focus, not stock biographies.

### 4. Earn reviews the compliant way

Ask every patient, consistently, without filtering for happy ones or offering incentives. Respond to reviews without confirming that the reviewer is a patient or discussing their care, a common way practices create privacy problems for themselves. Never buy or seed fake reviews: a fake-review story is exactly the kind of negative, highly retrievable content that ends up summarized in answers about you.

### 5. Check that AI crawlers can actually read your site

Review your robots.txt and any security or bot-blocking settings on your host or CDN. Blocking a training crawler is a legitimate choice; blocking the search crawlers that power AI answers removes you from those answers. Make sure core information (services, locations, hours, insurance) renders as text in the page, not inside images, PDFs, or widgets that only load after a script runs.

### 6. Get mentioned where patients and engines look

Local news coverage, specialty association directories, hospital affiliation pages, community health articles, and reputable "best of" lists in your city all feed the prominence signals Google describes and the source sets AI assistants retrieve. ## Does AI search change how healthcare paid search works?

Yes, at the margin, but it doesn't replace it. Paid search still captures patients at the moment they decide to book, and for high-intent queries like "urgent care open now" the ad and the map pack still do most of the work. What changes is the research phase before that moment, and that has two consequences for paid media.

First, the patients who click your ads are increasingly pre-sold or pre-filtered. They may have already seen your practice named in an AI answer, which tends to make branded and navigational searches more valuable and generic research queries less so. That is one reason healthcare search accounts plateau, which we covered in [what happens when healthcare paid search stops scaling](/blog/healthcare-paid-search-scaling/).

Second, the landing page has to do more of the confirming. A patient who arrives having already read a summary of your practice is checking whether what they heard is true: the clinician, the insurance, the availability. If your landing page contradicts the answer they just read, or makes them hunt for the detail they came to verify, they leave. ## How do you measure AI search's impact on new patients?

You measure it imperfectly, by combining a few partial signals, because AI answers that don't produce a click leave almost no trace in standard analytics.

- **Ask patients directly.** Add "How did you hear about us?" to your intake form or booking flow, with AI assistants (ChatGPT, Google's AI answers, and similar) as explicit options alongside Google search, referral, and social. Self-reported data is noisy, but it is the only way to see the zero-click influence at all.
- **Watch Search Console, not just rankings.** Google says traffic from AI Overviews and AI Mode is included in the Search Console Performance report under the "Web" search type. Track impressions and clicks on your service and location pages over time, and watch for a widening gap between impressions and clicks.
- **Track referral traffic from AI assistants.** Some assistants pass referrer data when a patient clicks a cited link. Set up a channel grouping in your analytics so those visits aren't buried in "referral" or "direct."
- **Run a monthly answer check.** Ask the major AI assistants the ten questions your patients ask about your specialty and city. Record who gets named, what is said about you, and which sources the answer cites. That source list is your to-do list.

Keep the measurement HIPAA-aware. Survey responses and analytics events should never carry health information into third-party ad or analytics tools, and "how did you hear about us" should be stored with intake data under the same controls. ## Where should a practice start?

Start with the monthly answer check, because it tells you where you actually stand. Then fix the listings and the missing answers on your site before doing anything more ambitious.

AI search hasn't changed what patients want from a provider. They still want someone qualified, nearby or available virtually, covered by their insurance, and trusted by other patients. What has changed is who does the first round of comparison. Increasingly it is a machine reading your public information, and it can only recommend what it can find, read, and trust.
