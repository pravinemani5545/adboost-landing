Open almost any healthcare Google Ads account and the best-looking campaign is the brand campaign. Cheap clicks, a conversion rate that dwarfs everything else, a cost per patient that makes the rest of the account look broken. It is also the campaign people are most suspicious of, because the obvious question is hard to shake: these patients already typed your name. Would they not have clicked the free listing right underneath?

The honest answer is "sometimes," and the difference is worth real money. This post covers what healthcare branded search ads actually do, what the research says, when paying for your own name protects patients and when it mostly claims credit, how to test which one you are in, and how to set up and report a brand campaign so it can't distort the rest of your paid search strategy.

## Should healthcare brands bid on their own name?

Most healthcare brands should run a branded search campaign, but on a short leash: separated from non-brand, capped in cost, and tested for incrementality rather than trusted on its reported numbers. Whether it is worth what you pay depends on who else is showing up when patients search your name.

Brand bidding is really two decisions: a default (usually yes) and a measurement question (how much is it really worth?) that most accounts never ask, even though reported brand returns are almost always inflated.

## What does a branded search ad actually do?

A branded search ad buys you control of the top of the results page for people who already know your name. Whether that control is worth paying for depends on what would sit there if you left.

A brand ad gives a clinic or telehealth brand three things the organic listing can't guarantee:

- **The top position.** On many brand queries, ads appear above your organic result. If a competitor, a directory, or a marketplace is bidding on your name, the first thing a ready-to-book patient sees may be someone else.
- **Message control.** You decide the headline, the offer, and the link. Organic snippets are written by Google from your page, and they don't always say "accepting new patients" or "same-week appointments" when that is what the searcher needs to hear.
- **Routing.** Sitelinks can send a patient straight to booking, insurance information, locations, or a specific service line instead of your homepage. For a multi-location group, the ad can point to the right clinic.

What a brand ad does not do is create demand. Every patient it reaches already knows your name, so it collects credit for patients that something else (a referral, a physician, a social ad, word of mouth) actually produced.

## What does the research say about branded search incrementality?

The best-known evidence points in both directions. A large field experiment at eBay found brand-keyword ads produced almost no short-term benefit, while Google's own pause studies found many paid clicks disappear when ads are turned off. Both can be true, because they describe very different search results pages.

The eBay study, published by Blake, Nosko, and Tadelis, switched off brand-keyword ads on some search engines and found that nearly all of the traffic simply moved to the organic listing ([NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20171)). eBay had an overwhelmingly strong brand, a dominant organic result, and, critically, almost no one else competing for its name.

Google's research team, analysing hundreds of pause studies, reported that the share of ad clicks that were incremental depended heavily on where the advertiser ranked organically: about 50% when the advertiser held the top organic position, 82% when it ranked second to fourth, and 96% when it ranked fifth or lower ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/impact-of-organic-ranking-on-ad-click-incrementality/)). Two caveats matter. That study is from 2012 and was published by the company selling the ads, and it covered paid search clicks generally, not brand terms specifically.

The useful takeaway is the mechanism, not a number: brand ads are most incremental when your organic listing is weak or contested, and least incremental when you own the page and nobody else bids. Only a test in your own data tells you where you sit.

## When does brand bidding protect demand for healthcare brands?

Brand bidding protects real patients when something else would take the top of the page if you stepped away. In healthcare, that happens more often than in most categories, because patients searching a provider's name are often still comparing.

Where a brand campaign usually earns its spend:

- **Competitors bid on your name.** Telehealth platforms, competing clinics, and national chains in the same specialty sometimes bid on local practice names and on each other. If a patient searching your name sees a competitor's ad first, some share will click it.
- **Directories and aggregators sit on your brand page.** Booking platforms, review sites, and provider directories often rank for practice and physician names, and some advertise. A patient who books through a third party may still become your patient, but you may pay a fee, lose the relationship, or lose them to the next listing on that platform.
- **Your organic listing is weak.** A new brand, a recent rebrand, a name shared with an unrelated business, or a website that ranks below directories for its own name all make the paid listing far more valuable.
- **Patients search brand plus intent.** Queries like "[clinic] new patient appointment," "[brand] cost," or "[brand] insurance" are high intent and specific. An ad that answers the exact question, with a sitelink to the right page, is worth more than a generic organic snippet.
- **You run multiple locations or service lines.** "[Brand] near me" and "[brand] + city" searches benefit from an ad that routes to the right location page, which the organic result may not do.

The quickest way to see whether any of this applies is Google's [auction insights report](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2579754?hl=en), which shows which other advertisers appear in your brand auctions, how often they overlap with you, and how often they show above you. If the report is empty or nearly empty, the protection argument is weak. If several competitors show up regularly, it is strong.

## When does a brand campaign just claim credit?

A brand campaign mostly claims credit when you rank first organically, nobody else bids on your name, and the patients clicking your ad would have clicked your organic result a few pixels lower. In that case you are paying for clicks you would have received free.

Warning signs:

- **Auction insights shows no meaningful competition** on your core brand terms.
- **Your organic result is first and your Google Business Profile dominates** the right-hand side or the map results.
- **Brand conversions rise every time other channels grow.** When a social or video campaign scales and brand search conversions jump in step, much of that is demand created upstream and collected by the brand campaign. We covered how that shows up in reporting in our guide to [healthcare marketing attribution](/blog/healthcare-marketing-attribution/).
- **Brand ROAS props up the whole account.** If search only looks healthy because brand is mixed in, brand is masking a non-brand problem.

None of these mean you should switch the campaign off tomorrow. They mean its reported return is not its real return, and the gap is worth measuring.

## How do you test whether your brand campaign is incremental?

Pause or reduce brand spend in a controlled way, then measure total brand-driven patients (paid plus organic) against a comparison period or a set of holdout markets. If organic absorbs almost all of the lost paid volume, the campaign was mostly claiming credit.

A practical sequence for a clinic group or telehealth brand:

1. **Check competition first.** If auction insights shows heavy competitor overlap, a full pause is risky; test a partial reduction instead.
2. **Pick the design.** Multi-location practices and state-licensed telehealth brands can usually run a geo holdout: pause brand in a matched set of markets, keep it running elsewhere. Single-location practices typically use a time-based on/off test with a clean comparison period.
3. **Measure in your own records.** Use new patients from your scheduling system or EHR, not platform conversions. Pair it with Google Search Console brand clicks so you can see how much traffic moved to organic.
4. **Run long enough.** Two to four weeks is a common minimum for brand tests. Avoid holidays, launches, and periods when other channels are changing.
5. **Read the result as incremental cost per patient.** Divide brand spend by the patients you actually lost when it was off, not by the patients the platform reported.

Here is a worked example with round numbers that are purely illustrative, not benchmarks. A multi-location clinic spends $6,000 a month on brand search, and Google Ads reports 300 new-patient bookings from it, a $20 reported cost per patient. In a four-week geo holdout, markets without brand ads lost bookings equivalent to 40 patients a month across the footprint, with most of the remaining paid traffic shifting to the organic listing. The incremental cost per patient is $6,000 ÷ 40, or $150. If that is within the clinic's allowable acquisition cost, the campaign stays on and only the reported number changes. If not, the budget gets trimmed and moves to channels that create demand.

## How should a healthcare brand campaign be set up?

Keep brand in its own campaign with its own budget, fence non-brand and automated campaigns away from brand queries, cap what you pay per click, and point every ad at the page that answers the query.

The setup that keeps brand honest:

- **Separate brand from non-brand.** Brand, non-brand, and competitor terms each get their own campaign. We lay out the full tiering in our [Google Ads playbook for telehealth](/blog/google-ads-telehealth-strategy/).
- **Use brand settings in both directions.** Google's [brand settings](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/13721847?hl=en) let you apply brand inclusions to a Search campaign so it serves only on your brand, and brand exclusions to Search and Performance Max campaigns so they stay off it. Google also notes that brand settings limit traffic by design, so apply them deliberately. Without exclusions, automated campaigns tend to pick up brand queries and report them as their own performance.
- **Control cost.** Brand clicks are usually cheap until a competitor shows up. A target impression share goal with a sensible maximum CPC limit, or a manual bid cap, stops a bidding war from quietly turning your cheapest campaign into an expensive one.
- **Write ads for patients who already chose you.** Lead with what they need next: booking, accepted insurance, locations, hours, telehealth availability. Use sitelinks to route, not to decorate.
- **Remember policy applies to every ad.** Google's [healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031?hl=en), including certification requirements for categories like prescription drugs, telemedicine, and addiction services, applies to your brand ads exactly as it does to non-brand. A brand campaign is not a policy loophole.
- **Keep conversion tracking HIPAA-aware.** Brand-plus-service queries can reveal what a patient is looking for. Conversion events sent back to ad platforms should describe actions, such as a booked or attended appointment, never conditions or treatment details.

## Should healthcare brands bid on competitor names?

Bidding on competitor names is allowed and can work, but it is usually expensive, low-converting, and only worth doing when your landing page genuinely wins the comparison. Treat it as a separate experiment, never a default.

Google's [trademark policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6118?hl=en) does not restrict trademarks as keywords, so a competitor can legally bid on your name in Google Ads and you can bid on theirs. Trademarks in ad text are a different matter: a trademark owner can file a complaint, and Google restricts ads from direct competitors that use the trademark in the ad copy, or that use it in a confusing or misleading way.

For healthcare specifically, three cautions apply:

- **Patient trust is fragile.** A patient searching for their own physician who lands on a competitor's page can feel misled. Ads should clearly identify who you are.
- **Relevance works against you.** Your ad and page are less relevant to someone else's name, which tends to raise click costs and lower conversion rates.
- **Measure it on incremental patients, not clicks.** Competitor campaigns can look busy and produce very little. Judge them on new patients and marginal cost per patient, the same way you would any [paid search scaling](/blog/healthcare-paid-search-scaling/) test.

## How should you report branded search?

Report brand search as its own line, never blended into non-brand search or channel ROAS. Brand performance tells you how much existing demand you are capturing, not how well your advertising is creating new patients.

A simple reporting structure:

- **Brand, non-brand, and competitor reported separately**, each with spend, new patients, and cost per patient.
- **Brand judged against its incremental cost per patient** from your most recent test, with the reported number shown beside it so nobody mistakes one for the other.
- **[Blended CAC](/glossary/blended-cac/) as the headline business metric**, so that shifting budget away from brand and toward demand creation shows up in the number that matters.
- **Branded search volume tracked as a demand signal.** Rising brand impressions after launching video or social is one of the better signs that upstream channels are working.
- **A retest on a cadence**, because competitive pressure on your name changes.

## The bottom line on healthcare brand bidding

Healthcare brands should usually bid on their own name, but they should pay for protection, not for credit.
