## Why do GLP-1 ad accounts get banned?

Five triggers account for almost every GLP-1 account shutdown we've audited: drug-name copy, transformation imagery on prescription offers, personal-attributes violations, unsubstantiated outcome claims, and landing page mismatch. The [state-AG pressure that reshaped Meta's GLP-1 rules](/blog/glp1-meta-ad-policy-state-ag-2026/) explains why enforcement tightened when it did.

1. **Drug-name targeting in ad copy.** Naming semaglutide or tirzepatide in an ad promoting your compounded or branded program reads as prescription drug promotion. Meta requires certification for that, and uncertified accounts get flagged fast.
2. **Transformation imagery on prescription offers.** Until July 2026, Meta's Health and Wellness standard banned side-by-side weight-loss comparisons, and many of the shutdowns we audited trace back to that rule. Meta deleted it in July 2026, but prescription weight-loss ads follow its Drugs and Pharmaceuticals standard, which says nothing about imagery either way. We still keep before/after and split-frame creative off Rx GLP-1 accounts: that is our recommendation, not Meta's written rule. Google's policies do not mention before/after imagery; its [unreliable claims policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936857) bars "claims of unrealistic weight loss within a specific time frame or requiring little effort".
3. **Personal attributes violations.** Copy that implies knowledge of the viewer's weight ("Struggling to lose those 30 lbs?") violates Meta's [personal attributes policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/), which bars ads that "assert or imply" a person's "physical or mental health (including medical conditions)". It is one of the most common and least understood triggers.
4. **Unsubstantiated outcome claims.** "Lose 15% of body weight" without clinical framing and disclaimers is a claims violation, even when the number comes from a real trial.
5. **Landing page mismatch.** The ad passes review, but the lander makes claims the ad didn't. Platforms crawl your landers. This is the silent killer.

Here's the same list as a working reference: what trips review, and what to run instead.

| Ban trigger | What flags it | Compliant alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Drug names in ad copy | Reads as prescription drug promotion without certification | Advertise the service: "talk to a licensed provider about medical weight-loss options" |
| Transformation imagery on Rx offers | No longer a written Meta rule since July 2026, but Rx GLP-1 ads follow Meta's Drugs and Pharmaceuticals standard, which is silent on imagery, so we treat it as a risk | Outcome data visualizations; patient stories built on energy and routine |
| Personal-attributes copy | Second-person references to the viewer's weight or health status | Program-first framing: "a medical program built around your metabolism" |
| Unsubstantiated outcome claims | Outcome percentages with no clinical framing or disclaimer | Cited trial data with the source and disclaimer inside the creative file |
| Landing page mismatch | Platform crawlers find claims on the lander that the ad never made | Audit the lander against the same policy checklist as the ad |

## What changed for GLP-1 ads in Meta's July 2026 update?

Meta revised its [Health and Wellness ad standard](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/) in July 2026. For GLP-1 brands the change is narrow:

- **Removed:** the ban on "Side-by-side comparison after the use of a product or transformation for weight loss", and the wording about ads that "imply or attempt to generate negative self-perception".
- **Added:** weight-loss ads can't contain "statements of inferiority about physical appearance", and health ads can't use clickbait such as "promises of specific outcomes within a set timeframe without disclaimers or qualifiers".
- **Unchanged:** weight-loss ads must target people 18 or older, and "For weight loss products that require prescription", Meta points to its [Drugs and Pharmaceuticals standard](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drugs-pharmaceuticals/): LegitScript certification, Meta authorization, US, Canada or New Zealand targeting, 18+. That standard last changed in February 2025 and says nothing about imagery.

So the July change clearly loosened the rules for non-prescription weight-loss products. For prescription GLP-1 ads, Meta's text does not say whether the Health and Wellness imagery allowance applies, so we plan as if it does not. The full list of deleted and added text, and how to tell which standard applies to your offer, is in our [summary of Meta's July 2026 change](/advertising/#meta-july-2026).

## What does Meta actually require for GLP-1 ads in 2026?

Meta requires telehealth providers and online pharmacies to be actively certified with [LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/) and to obtain Meta's authorization before running prescription drug ads, targeting only the US, Canada or New Zealand and people 18 or older. Without both, you're limited to advertising the *service* (medical consultations, provider access), not the medication: Meta's [Drugs and Pharmaceuticals standard](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drugs-pharmaceuticals/) says ads that "Promote telehealth services generally" need no written authorization. That's the load-bearing sentence in Meta's health and pharmacy framework, and it sits inside the broader [ad standards](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/) that govern personal attributes, sensational content, and claims. We keep a current breakdown of the policy line in our [GLP-1 on Meta guide](/advertising/glp-1-on-meta/).

The service-versus-medication distinction is the entire game:

- **Compliant:** "Talk to a licensed provider about medical weight-loss options."
- **Non-compliant:** "Get semaglutide delivered to your door for $199/month."

Google runs a parallel system through its [healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031). In the US, Canada and New Zealand you may use prescription drug terms such as semaglutide in ad text and landing pages without certification, but you must be certified to keyword-target them ([Google's restricted drug terms policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15595717)), and telemedicine providers that prescribe need LegitScript and then Google's own certification. The enforcement styles differ (Google tends to disapprove ad by ad while Meta escalates to account-level action faster), but the direction is identical: certify first, or stay strictly on the service side of the line. The GLP-1 drug-term rules are in our [GLP-1 on Google guide](/advertising/glp-1-on-google/), and the certification order is on [telehealth on Google](/advertising/telehealth-on-google/).

## How do you write GLP-1 ad creative that converts *and* passes review?

Build angles that are native to the constraints: sell the protocol, the provider, and the life after, never the drug itself. The mistake most agencies make is treating compliance as a legal filter applied after the creative process; that produces beige, dead ads. The teams that win in this category start from the constraint:

- **Lead with the protocol, not the drug.** "A medical program built around your metabolism" outperforms drug-name copy long-term anyway, because it frames a defensible brand instead of a commodity price war.
- **Use clinician authority.** Provider-led UGC (real MDs and NPs explaining how the program works) is the highest-performing compliant format we run. It converts because it borrows trust, and it passes because it's educational.
- **Sell the second-order outcome.** Energy, confidence, bloodwork, longevity. You avoid the personal-attributes minefield entirely when the creative is about what life looks like after, not the viewer's current body.
- **Put claims on a compliance rail.** Every stat gets a source, a disclaimer, and clinical framing, in the creative file itself, not just the lander. This is the same substantiation standard the [FTC's health products compliance guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) holds advertisers to, so building it into production isn't overhead; it's insurance against a second regulator.

## What should your landing page do differently?

Treat the lander as part of the ad, because platform review does. The compliant GLP-1 lander in 2026:

- States eligibility requirements and "prescription only if appropriate" language above the fold
- Shows real pricing with the consultation unbundled from medication
- Carries medical disclaimers and provider licensing information in crawlable HTML (not images)
- Keeps before/after galleries off prescription GLP-1 pages and uses outcome data visualizations instead (our recommendation; Meta's Drugs and Pharmaceuticals standard is silent on imagery)

We've seen accounts survive review sweeps purely because their landers were audit-clean while competitors' weren't. The inverse is also true: a clean ad pointing at a lander that promises what the ad carefully avoided is the single most common way "compliant" accounts still get flagged.

## What do you do if your account is already banned?

Run a five-step recovery process: diagnose, fix, document, appeal, and only if the account is unrecoverable, rebuild with certification first. This is the sequence AdBoost Health runs when a partner arrives with a disabled account (our [Meta appeal playbook for disabled accounts](/blog/meta-ad-account-banned-health-brand/) goes deeper):

1. **Diagnose the actual violation.** Pull every rejection notice and policy citation from the account, then review the last 90 days of creative and the landing pages against the trigger table above. Most teams appeal blind; you can't fix what you haven't named.
2. **Fix everything before appealing.** Scrub drug names from live and paused ads, pull transformation assets from Rx offers, rewrite lander claims, add missing disclaimers. Appealing an un-fixed violation burns your credibility with review teams and makes every subsequent appeal slower.
3. **Assemble the documentation.** LegitScript certification, provider licenses, claim substantiation files, business verification. Appeals with documentation attached resolve materially faster than "we believe this was a mistake" tickets.
4. **Submit one factual, specific appeal.** State what was flagged, what you changed, and what the attached documentation proves. Resist the urge to re-submit the same appeal repeatedly: identical repeat appeals train the system toward auto-denial.
5. **If the account is unrecoverable, rebuild properly.** A new Business Manager, warmed gradually with non-health spend, certification completed *before* the first health ad runs. Not the growth-hack route of cycling fresh accounts: that triggers circumvention policies and ends with a permanent platform ban.
